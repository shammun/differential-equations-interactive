The Bobbing Buoy

An interactive companion to the MITx 18.03x buoy problems. Orange controls are things you set; teal boxes are what the model computes; purple buttons do things.

The problem

A cylindrical buoy of radius r, height h and density half that of water floats in still water. Let x be the depth of the buoy's bottom below the surface, measured downward. You hold the buoy with its bottom at the surface (x = 0) and let go. How deep does it float, how fast does it bob, and what does the simple model leave out? Work through the four tabs in order.

What the model computes

Mass of buoy m = ρbπr²h
Displaced water mwd = ρwπr²x
Gravity FG = +mg (down)
Buoyancy FB = −mwdg (up)
Net force FG + FB
Acceleration ẍ = net / m

Two forces act: gravity pulls down, and the water pushes up with a force equal to the weight of the water the buoy displaces (Archimedes). Drag the depth slider and watch them fight.

  • Slide x until the net force reads 0. That depth is where the buoy floats.
  • Change the radius r. Forces change, but ẍ does not — r cancels.
  • Set ρb = 0.9: the buoy floats much deeper (xeq = 0.9h).

Net force as a function of depth

Newton's law: m ẍ = mg − ρwπr²x·g. Dividing by m = ρbπr²h: ẍ = g − (g ρw / h ρb) x. With ρb = ½ρw this is ẍ = g − (2g/h) x. The force is a straight line in x, crossing zero at the floating depth, so above it the buoy is pushed down and below it pushed up — a restoring force, exactly what makes an oscillator.

Released from rest with its bottom at the surface (x = 0, ẋ = 0). The equation ẍ + ωn²x = g is a harmonic oscillator whose centre is the floating depth, not zero. Left figure: the buoy itself. Right: its depth against time — the cosine curve worked out in the steps below.

  • Double h. The period grows by √2 (T ∝ √h) — count it on the teal bracket.
  • Set x(0) equal to xeq. Amplitude 0: the buoy just sits there.
  • Lower g to 1.6 (the Moon). Same floating depth, much slower bob.

Phase plane: depth against velocity

With no damping the point goes round the same closed loop forever, centred on xeq: energy is conserved, so the buoy never settles. Compare with the spiral in tab 4.

      What is missing from the model?

      Regime
      ωn = √(2g/h)
      Critical damping bc = 2ωn
      Equation ẍ + bẋ + ωn²x = g

      A real buoy stops bobbing. Add a force that opposes velocity, −b ẋ, to the ρb = ½ buoy and compare with the frictionless model (grey curve).

      • Nudge b just above 0: the bobs keep their rhythm but shrink inside the teal envelope.
      • Set b near bc: the buoy sinks to its floating depth with no overshoot at all.
      • Push b past bc: it still doesn't overshoot, but takes longer to settle.

      Phase plane: closed loop vs spiral

      Without damping the solution is x = (h/2)(1 − cos ωnt) and the amplitude never shrinks: the buoy bobs forever. Real water produces drag and turbulence, which appear in the equation as b ẋ. The characteristic roots become r = −b/2 ± √(b²/4 − ωn²): complex (underdamped, spiral), repeated (critical), or real (overdamped, no crossings).
