Beats — an interactive tour

A rebuild of the classic 18.03 “Beats” mathlet, plus a guided walk through the mathematics behind every pixel of it. The sliders are shared across all tabs: change ω, A or φ anywhere and every graph, formula and worked number updates together.

What you are looking at

Two pure tones are being added, sample by sample. The green wave f(t) = sin(t) is fixed. The magenta wave g(t) = A sin(ωt − φ) has three dials: an amplitude A = , a circular frequency ω = , and a phase lag φ = .

When the two frequencies are close but not equal — right now the gap is ω − 1 = — the waves drift slowly in and out of step. Where they agree they reinforce (the sum reaches about 1 + A = ); where they disagree they cancel (down to |1 − A| = ). That slow swelling and fading of loudness is a beat — the throbbing wah‑wah‑wah you hear when two guitar strings are almost, but not quite, in tune.

With the current sliders a full beat lasts 2π / |ω − 1| = , so the [0, 60π] window holds about beats. Turn on Envelope to see the loudness curve drawn in gray, then visit the next tabs to see where all of these numbers come from.

Color code used everywhere: green = f, magenta = g, blue = the sum x, gray = envelope. Orange chips are values you control; teal chips are values computed from them.

Step 0 — the two ingredients, one dial at a time

Before adding anything, let’s be sure of what each wave is by itself. A sinusoid is completely described by three numbers, and each slider changes exactly one of them:

f(t) = sin(t)   — amplitude 1, circular frequency 1, no phase lag g(t) = A sin(ωt − φ)  — amplitude , circular frequency , phase lag
Move the pointer over the graph to sample both waves at one instant.

Amplitude A — how tall

sin(·) only ever outputs numbers between −1 and 1. Multiplying by A stretches that range to [−A, +A]. With A = the magenta peaks sit at ± — the dashed orange lines in the graph. A changes nothing about timing: peaks stay exactly where they were, they just get taller or shorter.

Circular frequency ω — how fast

Think of ω as a speed: the angle fed into the sine grows by ω radians per unit of time. One full cycle needs the angle to grow by 2π radians, so the period is

Tg = 2π / ω = 6.283 /  = compare: f has ω = 1, so Tf = 2π ≈ 6.28 (that is why the t-axis is marked in multiples of π)

Equivalently: in the time green completes one cycle, magenta completes ω = cycles. In the mathlet’s [0, 60π] window, f fits exactly 30 cycles while g fits 30ω = cycles.

Phase lag φ — how late

The “− φ” inside the sine holds the wave back: g doesn’t begin its upward zero-crossing until the angle ωt − φ reaches 0, i.e. at

ωt − φ = 0  ⇒  t = φ / ω =  /  = marked by the dashed magenta line in the graph — the whole wave is slid right by this amount

Note the division by ω: a lag of φ radians of angle translates into φ/ω units of time, because a faster wave burns through radians faster.

Try it. (1) Drag A to 0.3 — magenta shrinks, timing untouched. (2) Drag ω to 1.0 — both waves now have the same wavelength and never drift apart (this kills the beats in tab 1!). (3) Drag φ from 0 to 2 and watch the magenta wave slide right without changing shape.

Step 1 — adding the waves is just adding heights

At every instant t, the blue curve of tab 1 is nothing more than two numbers added together: x(t) = f(t) + g(t). Hover over the graph below and the readout does that arithmetic for you at the crosshair. Everything else in this subject is about understanding the pattern those sums make.

This tab pins A = 1 (equal loudness), because the classic factoring trick below needs equal amplitudes. Tab 4 removes that restriction. Your ω and φ sliders still apply here.

Step 2 — the sum-to-product identity

The whole phenomenon of beats falls out of one trig identity:

sin α + sin β = 2 sin((α+β)/2) cos((α−β)/2) “sum of two sines = (sine at the average angle) × (cosine at half the disagreement)”

Substitute our two angles, α = t and β = ωt − φ, then simplify the averages:

x(t) = sin(t) + sin(ωt − φ)      = 2 cos(½[(ω−1)t − φ]) · sin(½[(ω+1)t − φ]) with your sliders: x(t) = 2 cos(  t − ) · sin(  t − )

Step 3 — read the two factors as “volume knob × tone”

Look at the two frequencies that appeared:

  • The sine factor oscillates at the average frequency (ω+1)/2 = — nearly as fast as the originals. This is the fast wiggle you see (and the pitch you would hear).
  • The cosine factor oscillates at half the difference (ω−1)/2 = — much slower. Over any one fast wiggle it barely moves, so it acts as a slowly turning volume knob multiplying the tone.

The graph below shows the factorization visually: slow knob on top, fast tone in the middle, and their product at the bottom — which is exactly the blue curve of tab 1 (for A = 1).

Step 4 — how long is a beat?

Loudness peaks whenever the volume knob hits ±1, i.e. whenever cos(½[(ω−1)t − φ]) = ±1. Consecutive peaks of |cos| are π radians of its argument apart, so consecutive loud moments are separated by

½ (ω−1) ΔT = π  ⇒  ΔT = 2π / (ω−1) = 6.283 /  = so the [0, 60π] window of tab 1 contains 60π ÷ ΔT ≈ beats — count the gray lobes!
Try it. Drag ω toward 1 (say 1.10): the knob turns extremely slowly — beats become long and languid (ΔT = 2π/0.10 ≈ 63 = 20π: one beat fills a whole zoomed window). Drag ω far from 1 (say 1.9): beats get short and fast. At ω = 1 exactly the knob freezes — constant loudness, no beats at all.

Step 5 — unequal amplitudes: think of rotating arrows

The factoring trick of tab 3 needs equal amplitudes. For general A = there is a picture that works every time: each sinusoid is the height of the tip of a rotating arrow (a phasor).

  • Green arrow: length 1, angle t — it turns at 1 radian per unit time. Its tip-height is sin(t) = f(t).
  • Magenta arrow: length A, angle ωt − φ, glued tail-to-tip onto the green one — it turns at ω = rad per unit time and starts φ = radians behind.
  • Blue arrow: from the origin to the final tip. Heights simply add, so its tip-height is x(t) = f(t) + g(t) — the blue dot tracing the curve on the right.

Step 6 — the angle between the arrows is everything

Both arrows spin fast, but what decides the length of the blue arrow is only the gap angle between them:

gap(t) = (ωt − φ) − t = (ω − 1) t − φ it grows at the slow rate ω − 1 = rad per unit time — one full lap of the gap every 2π/(ω−1) = , the beat period again

When the gap is 0 the arrows point the same way and lengths add: 1 + A = . When the gap is π they point opposite ways and lengths subtract: |1 − A| = . In between, the law of cosines gives the length exactly:

E(t)² = 1² + A² + 2A cos(gap)   ⇒   E(t) = √( 1 + A² + 2A cos((ω−1)t − φ) ) with your sliders: E(t) = √( +  cos(  t − ) ), swinging between and

This is the gray envelope of tab 1. The blue curve is the fast rotation filling the inside; ±E(t) is the slowly breathing boundary it can never cross. The first loudest instant after t = 0 is where the gap first returns to zero:

(ω−1) t − φ = 0  ⇒  t = φ / (ω−1) =  /  = so φ does not change the size or spacing of the beats — it only slides the whole envelope in time

Sanity checks that fall out for free: with A = 1, √(2 + 2cos θ) = 2|cos(θ/2)| — exactly the volume knob of tab 3. And with ω = 1 the gap never moves, so E is a constant √(1+A²+2A cos φ) = : two equal-frequency sines always merge into one steady sinusoid — no beats.

Try it. Press Play and watch the gap angle open and close. Then drag A to 0.5: the arrows can no longer cancel, so the quiet parts of the beat bottom out at |1−A| = 0.5 instead of touching zero — check the envelope in tab 1.

From formula to pixels

Everything you see is produced by one loop: pick many values of t, compute x(t) = sin(t) + A sin(ωt − φ) for each, convert every (t, x) pair to a pixel position, and connect the dots with short line segments. This tab shows that machinery — including how it can lie to you.

Step 7 — the coordinate map

A canvas has its origin at the top-left, with y growing downward — the opposite of math convention. Two linear maps translate between the worlds (padL = 44 px of margin holds the y-labels; the plot area is px wide and px tall on your screen right now):

px(t) = padL + ( t / tmax ) · plotWidth — fraction of the window, scaled to pixels py(x) = midY − ( x / 2 ) · ( plotHeight / 2 ) — minus flips y; the 2 is the axis limit ±2

The crosshair readout runs this in reverse: from the mouse pixel it computes t = (mouseX − padL)/plotWidth · tmax, then evaluates the formula — it reads mathematics, not pixels.

Step 8 — the drawing loop (the real code)

This is the exact routine this page uses for every curve:

function drawCurve(ctx, map, fn, color, width, N){
  // N sample points, evenly spaced across the time window
  ctx.strokeStyle = color;  ctx.lineWidth = width;
  ctx.beginPath();
  for (let i = 0; i <= N; i++){
    const t = i / N * map.tMax;      // math world
    const v = fn(t);                 // x(t) = sin(t) + A·sin(ωt − φ)
    const X = map.px(t), Y = map.py(v);  // pixel world
    i === 0 ? ctx.moveTo(X, Y) : ctx.lineTo(X, Y);
  }
  ctx.stroke();
}

Step 9 — how many samples are enough? (aliasing)

Straight segments only look like a smooth curve if the samples are dense compared with the fastest wiggle. The fastest component has frequency max(1, ω) = , so its period is 2π/max(1,ω) = time units, and the [0, 60π] window contains such cycles. With N samples you get N ÷ = samples per fastest cycle —

Drag N down. Below ~2 samples per cycle (the Nyquist limit) the dots genuinely cannot tell the true curve from a much slower impostor, and the plot draws confident nonsense — same formula, same dots, wrong story. This page uses N = 4200 for the 60π window (≈ samples per fastest cycle), far above danger. Dots are shown when N ≤ 260 so you can see exactly what the line is built from.

Step 10 — the remaining details

  • Envelope: drawn as two more curves, +E(t) and −E(t), with the formula from tab 4 — the gray lines are ordinary plots, not magic.
  • Zoom: only tmax changes (60π ↔ 20π); the same map formulas instantly re-spread the pixels. Nothing about x(t) is recomputed differently.
  • Sharpness: the canvas is rendered at devicePixelRatio × CSS size, so lines stay crisp on high-density screens.
  • Reactivity: every slider writes to one shared state object {ω, A, φ, zoom, envelope}; any change triggers one render() pass that redraws all visible canvases and refreshes every teal number on this page.
