1 · A phasor is just an arrow with a growing angle

Pin an arrow of length A at the centre of the circle and let its angle grow at a steady rate — ω radians every second. At time t its angle is θ = ωt − φ. Worked, with ω = 1.57 rad/s and φ = 1.57: at t = 0 s, θ = 0×1.57 − 1.57 = −1.57 (arrow points straight down); at t = 1 s, θ = 1.57 − 1.57 = 0 (arrow points right); at t = 2 s, θ = 3.14 − 1.57 = +1.57 (straight up). Steady spin — nothing else.

2 · The wave is the arrow's shadow — and there are two shadows

The one spinning arrow casts two shadows, and each is a wave. Its vertical shadow — the height of the tip above the midline — is A sin θ. Its horizontal shadow — how far the tip sits to the right of centre — is A cos θ. Use the Sine / Cosine / Both buttons to watch either shadow (or both) being read off the same arrow. Concrete check at θ = 0, arrow at 3 o'clock: height = sin 0 = 0, but horizontal reach = cos 0 = 1, the full radius. So sine peaks when the arrow points straight up (θ = π/2), while cosine peaks when it points right (θ = 0). “Vertical/highest = maximum” is a sine-only statement; for cosine, maximum means “fully to the right”. The two are one wave a quarter-turn apart: cos θ = sin(θ + π/2) — cosine is a sine with a π/2 head start. Everything else transfers identically for both: radius → amplitude, one lap → one period, spin rate → ω, head start −φ → phase.

3 · Why counterclockwise, not clockwise?

Because θ = ωt − φ increases with time, and by the standard convention increasing angles sweep counterclockwise from the 3 o'clock direction. Check it with the graph itself: when the arrow passes 3 o'clock (θ = 0), the height is 0 and, an instant later, sin θ is slightly positive — so the tip must move up from 3 o'clock. Up from 3 o'clock is counterclockwise. A clockwise arrow would move down from 3 o'clock and its height would trace A sin(−ωt − φ) — a mirror-image wave that disagrees with the graph. Tick the red checkbox in the Inputs card to see exactly that mismatch drawn live. (Try it with φ = 0. One curious exception: at φ = ±π/2 the wave is ∓A cos(ωt), which reads the same forwards and backwards in time, so its mirror lands exactly on top of it — the red dot still betrays the wrong direction, though.)

4 · So when would clockwise be right?

When the angle in the formula decreases with time. In tab 2 a rightward-travelling wave is y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ); watch one fixed point x₀ and its angle kx₀ − ωt + φ shrinks as t grows — a phasor for that point really does turn clockwise. Same physics, opposite sign in front of ωt. The rotation direction always just follows the sign of the ωt term: +ωt → counterclockwise, −ωt → clockwise. It is a bookkeeping convention, not a law of nature.

Three names for one fact

T, f and ω all answer "how fast does it repeat?" — they only differ in the unit of one cycle:

The conversion chain, with numbers

Take T = 4 s. Then f = 1/T = 1/4 = 0.25 Hz (a quarter cycle each second, so 4 s per cycle — reciprocals). One cycle is one full lap of the circle = 2π ≈ 6.283 radians, so ω = 2π × f = 6.283 × 0.25 = 1.571 rad/s, which is the same as ω = 2π/T. Multiply cycles-per-second by radians-per-cycle and you get radians-per-second — pure unit bookkeeping.

Why bother with ω at all?

Because sin() eats angles, not seconds or cycles. ω is the exchange rate that converts clock time into the angle sin() needs: after t seconds the accumulated angle is ωt radians. With ω = 1.571 rad/s, after t = 2 s the angle is 1.571 × 2 = 3.142 rad — exactly half a lap (π), i.e. half a cycle of the wave, as T = 4 s says it should be.
Phase lag φ
φ = π/2 rad = 90°
How far behind the reference the wave runs, measured as an angle. It must be an angle: ωt is in radians, and you can only subtract like from like. Negative φ = a phase lead.

1 · Two runners on the same circular track

Picture the reference wave and your wave as two runners going round the phasor circle at the same speed ω. Your runner started φ radians behind on the track, and can never catch up: at every instant, your runner's angle = (reference angle) − φ. That is literally the formula — sin(ωt − φ) versus sin(ωt). Turn on "Show reference wave" and watch the grey and blue arrows keep a fixed pink angle φ between them while both spin.

2 · Why φ must be an angle (and is "unitless")

Whatever sits inside sin( ) has to be an angle. ωt already is one: (rad/s) × s = rad. Since you can only subtract like from like, φ must also be in radians — and radians are a pure number (arc length ÷ radius), so φ carries no physical unit. The seconds version of the same shift is a different symbol: t₀ = φ/ω.

3 · Why positive φ means "later", step by step

The wave peaks when its angle reaches π/2. Without lag: ωt = π/2 → t = π/2ω. With lag: ωt − φ = π/2 → t = (π/2 + φ)/ω — a bigger t, so the peak happens later, and the whole graph slides right. Worked, with ω = 1.571 and φ = 1.571 (= π/2): peak without lag at t = 1.571/1.571 = 1 s; with lag at t = (1.571+1.571)/1.571 = 2 s. One second late — which is exactly t₀ = φ/ω = 1 s. The minus sign in sin(ωt − φ) is precisely what makes positive φ mean "delayed".

4 · φ as a fraction of a cycle

A whole cycle is 2π, so φ/2π tells you what fraction of a cycle the wave is behind — independent of ω. φ = π/2 → (π/2)/2π = 1/4 of a cycle, always. Special values (for the sine form used here):

Time lag t₀
t₀ = φ/ω = 1.57/1.57 = 1.00 s
The same lateness in seconds — rad ÷ (rad/s) = s. Try it: raise ω and the first peak slides left even though φ never moved, because t₀ = φ/ω shrinks.

1 · Converting angle-lateness into clock-lateness

φ says "behind by so many radians"; to turn that into seconds, divide by how many radians pass per second — which is ω. Hence t₀ = φ/ω. The units confirm it: rad ÷ (rad/s) = s. Worked: φ = 1.571 rad behind, spinning at ω = 1.571 rad/s → behind by 1.571/1.571 = 1.00 s. Now double the speed to ω = 3.142 with the same φ: t₀ = 1.571/3.142 = 0.50 s. Same angle behind, half the seconds behind, because everything happens twice as fast.

2 · The one-line proof: factor ω out

A sin(ωt − φ) = A sin( ω(t − φ/ω) ) = A sin( ω(t − t₀) ). Compare with the un-lagged A sin(ωt): it is the identical function with t replaced by t − t₀ — i.e. every event (peak, zero, trough) occurs t₀ seconds later. Factoring ω out of the bracket is the entire content of "t₀ = φ/ω". This is why the graph shows the time-lag arrow as a pure horizontal shift.

3 · Two rulers, one shift

The sideways shift can be quoted in either currency, and the fractions always agree: φ/2π (fraction of a cycle, in angle) = t₀/T (fraction of a period, in time). With φ = π/2, ω = 1.571: φ/2π = 0.25 and t₀/T = 1/4 = 0.25 ✓. The "Fraction of a cycle" tile below tracks this equality live as you drag any slider.
