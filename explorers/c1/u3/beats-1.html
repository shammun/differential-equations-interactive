What you are looking at

Two pure tones are playing. The green one never changes: it is sin t, one full oscillation every 2π ≈ 6.283 units of time. The magenta one is yours to tune. When its frequency ω sits close to 1, the two waves drift slowly in and out of step, and their sum swells and fades. That slow swelling is the beat — the thing a piano tuner listens for.

Everything on this screen is arithmetic you could do by hand at any single instant t: evaluate the green wave, evaluate the magenta wave, add the two numbers. The blue curve is that sum, done again at every pixel. The remaining tabs work through why the result looks the way it does.

02 — One wave and its three knobs

Before adding anything, get comfortable with what A, ω and φ each do to a single sine wave.

Start from the plainest possible wave, the green one in the instrument:

f(t) = sin(t)

It leaves 0 heading upward at t = 0, peaks at t = π/2 ≈ 1.571 with value 1, returns to 0 at t = π ≈ 3.142, bottoms out at −1, and is back where it started at t = 2π ≈ 6.283. That repeat distance is its period.

The magenta wave is the same shape with three modifications:

g(t) = A sin(ωt − φ)
Knob 1 — amplitude A

A multiplies the whole wave, so the peaks sit at A and the troughs at −A. It stretches the picture vertically and does nothing to the timing. At A = 0 the magenta wave disappears entirely.

Knob 2 — circular frequency ω

Inside the sine, t has been replaced by ωt, so the clock runs ω times faster. A full cycle now takes

period = 2πω  — at ω = 1.770 that is 2π / 1.770 = 3.550, about 1.130π.

So the magenta wave fits roughly 1.77 oscillations into every one of the green wave's. Bigger ω means a tighter, faster wave.

Knob 3 — phase lag φ

Subtracting φ delays the wave. Factor the ω out to see by how much:

A sin(ωt − φ) = A sin(ω(t − φω))

The whole wave slides right by φ/ω in time. With φ = 1.13 and ω = 1.770 that is 1.13 / 1.770 = 0.638 — a small nudge, about a fifth of the magenta wave's own period. Note that φ is measured in radians of the wave's own angle, so the same φ produces a smaller time shift when ω is large.

03 — Adding the two waves

Superposition is the least mysterious step in the whole picture: at each instant, add the two heights.

x(t) = sin(t) + A sin(ωt − φ)

There is no interaction between the waves and no hidden rule. Pick t = 2.00 with the default settings and do it by hand:

QuantityArithmetic
sin(2.00)= 0.909
sin(1.770 × 2.00 − 1.13)= sin(2.410) = 0.668
x(2.00)= 0.909 + 0.668 = 1.577

Repeat that at a few thousand closely spaced values of t, join the dots, and you have the blue curve.

When δ is near 0 the waves are momentarily in step and reinforce; near ±π they oppose and cancel. That single number is the whole story of the beat, and tab 5 turns it into a formula.

The special case that explains everything else

Set ω = 1 exactly in the demo above. The sum stops pulsing and becomes a plain sine wave of fixed height. That is not a coincidence:

sin t + A sin(t − φ) = C sin(t + ψ),    C = √1 + A² + 2A cos φ

Two waves of the same frequency always add up to a third wave of that same frequency; only the height and the timing change. With A = 1 and φ = 1.13, C = √(2 + 2cos 1.13) = 1.689 — a fixed height, no beating.

Beats only exist because ω ≠ 1. And keep that formula for C in view: in tab 5 the identical expression comes back with φ replaced by a slowly turning angle, and that is the envelope.

04 — Why a slow pulse appears

One trigonometric identity converts "two fast waves added" into "one fast wave, slowly amplitude-modulated". Take A = 1 here; tab 5 handles the general case.

The identity is the sum-to-product rule. It comes straight from the angle-addition formula:

sin(u + v) = sin u cos v + cos u sin v
sin(u − v) = sin u cos v − cos u sin v

Add those two lines; the cos u sin v terms cancel:

sin(u+v) + sin(u−v) = 2 sin u cos v

Now match this to our sum, whose two angles are α = t and β = ωt − φ. Solving α = u+v, β = u−v gives the half-sum and half-difference:

u = α+β2 = (1+ω)t − φ2      v = α−β2 = (1−ω)t + φ2
x(t) = 2 cos((1−ω)t + φ2) · sin((1+ω)t − φ2)

This is exact — no approximation anywhere. It says the sum is a product of two pieces, and their speeds are what matter:

  • The sine factor runs at the average frequency (1+ω)/2. At ω = 1.770 that is 1.385, so its period is 2π/1.385 = 4.537. This is the fast wiggle you see filling the blue curve.
  • The cosine factor runs at half the difference, |1−ω|/2 = 0.385. It changes so slowly that over one fast wiggle it is nearly constant, so it acts as a slowly varying amplitude.

How long is one beat?

The ear (and the eye) responds to the size of the amplitude, not its sign, so the relevant quantity is |2 cos(·)|. A cosine has period 2π, but its absolute value repeats twice as often. The slow factor's argument advances at rate |1−ω|/2, so:

beat period = 2π|1 − ω|  =  2π / 0.770 = 8.16 ≈ 2.60π at the default settings.

Two consequences worth holding onto. As ω → 1 the beat period blows up: the waves take forever to drift apart, and over a 20π window you may see no pulsing at all. As ω moves far from 1, the beat period shrinks until the "slow" factor is no longer slow compared with the wiggle, and the sum just looks like a complicated periodic mess rather than a throbbing tone.

The nodes — the instants where the sum momentarily vanishes — are where the cosine factor is zero, which happens when its argument equals π/2 plus a multiple of π. With ω = 1.770 and φ = 1.13 the first one lands at t = 5.548 ≈ 1.77π, and the rest follow every 8.16.

05 — The envelope, for any A and φ

When A ≠ 1 the neat product formula fails, because the two waves no longer cancel completely. Rotating arrows give the general answer in four steps.

The trick is to treat each sine as the vertical shadow of a rotating arrow. Since eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ, the imaginary part of eiθ is sin θ. So:

Step 1 — write the sum as one shadow
x(t) = Im[ eit + A ei(ωt−φ) ]

Two arrows: a unit arrow turning at rate 1, and an arrow of length A turning at rate ω, starting φ radians behind. Lay them tip to tail; the sum's shadow on the vertical axis is x(t).

Step 2 — factor out the average rotation

Both arrows share a common turning. Pull out the average phase Φ = ((1+ω)t − φ)/2 and let ψ = ((1−ω)t + φ)/2 be the half-difference — the same two quantities as in tab 4:

eit + A ei(ωt−φ) = eiΦ [ eiψ + A e−iψ ]
Step 3 — the bracket is a slowly changing complex number

Expand it into real and imaginary parts:

eiψ + A e−iψ = (1+A) cos ψ + i (1−A) sin ψ

Its length R and angle θ are

R² = (1+A)² cos²ψ + (1−A)² sin²ψ = 1 + A² + 2A cos 2ψ,   tan θ = 1−A1+A tan ψ

The middle step uses cos²ψ − sin²ψ = cos 2ψ, and 2ψ = (1−ω)t + φ.

Step 4 — read off the envelope

Taking the imaginary part of R ei(Φ+θ):

x(t) = R(t) sin(Φ(t) + θ(t)),     R(t) = √1 + A² + 2A cos((1−ω)t + φ)

Because a sine never exceeds 1 in size, |x(t)| ≤ R(t) always, with equality whenever the fast sine happens to hit ±1. That is exactly what "envelope" means, and it is what the Envelope checkbox in tab 1 draws, as ±R.

The formula is just the law of cosines for a triangle with sides 1 and A. The angle between the two arrows is δ(t) = (ω−1)t − φ, and it creeps around once every beat period. So:

When the arrows are…The envelope is…At A = 1
aligned (δ = 0)R = 1 + A   (largest)2.00
opposed (δ = π)R = |1 − A|   (smallest)0.00
at right anglesR = √(1 + A²)1.414

This also explains something you can check in tab 1: with A = 0.5 the sum never quite dies away, because the shorter arrow cannot cancel the longer one — the envelope's minimum is |1 − 0.5| = 0.5. Full cancellation, and the dramatic nodes in the original picture, require A = 1. Setting A = 0 makes R = 1 constant: one wave, no beat.

Finally, note where φ went. It appears inside R only as an additive shift of the slow angle, so changing φ slides the whole envelope left or right without changing its shape or its period. Changing ω, by contrast, changes how fast δ turns, and so changes the beat period itself.

06 — How the picture actually gets drawn

A canvas knows nothing about t or x. Every curve here is a few thousand short line segments, placed by two linear maps.

The plotting rectangle

Each screen reserves a margin for the axis labels; what is left is the plotting rectangle. For the top screen in tab 1, right now on your display:

The canvas is allocated at the device resolution and then scaled back down, so lines stay crisp on high-density screens. All the arithmetic below is done in CSS pixels; the scaling happens once, in the transform.

Time to horizontal position

The map is a straight proportion: how far along the t range you are is how far across the rectangle you go.

px = L + t − tmintmax − tmin · W

—

Value to vertical position

The same idea, flipped, because canvas y grows downward. With the vertical range fixed at [−2, 2]:

py = T + xmax − xxmax − xmin · H

—

The vertical range is deliberately fixed at ±2 and never rescaled — that is why turning A down makes the sum visibly shrink inside the frame instead of the axes zooming to fit it.

The pointer readout, running the map backwards

The t = and x = fields in tab 1 report the pointer's own position, not the curve's value. The code takes the mouse coordinates relative to the canvas and inverts the two formulas above:

t = tmin + px − LW(tmax − tmin)      x = xmax − py − TH(xmax − xmin)

The result is printed in units of π, which is why the readout says something like t = 13.24π: the raw number 41.59 is divided by π before display. The same t then draws the vertical guide line on both screens.

Sampling: how many points is enough?

The curve is drawn by stepping through N evenly spaced values of t, computing the function at each, and calling lineTo. Too few points and the fast wiggle is missed entirely — the drawn curve then shows a slow ripple that is not in the function at all. That is aliasing, the same effect that makes wagon wheels spin backwards on film.

The demand comes from the fastest thing on screen. For the sum, the fast factor has period 2π/((1+ω)/2). Over a window of 60π at ω = 1.77 that is about 41 full wiggles; with fewer than a few points per wiggle the picture is fiction. The instrument uses roughly three samples per horizontal pixel, so it is drawing well past what the display can resolve.

canvas.height = cssHeight * dpr;

// 2. frame: white plot rect, tick marks, the t axis at x = 0
drawFrame();

// 3. one path per curve, clipped to the rect
const N = Math.round(plotWidth * 3);
ctx.beginPath();
for (let i = 0; i <= N; i++) {
  const t = tmin + (tmax - tmin) * i / N;
  const x = Math.sin(t) + A * Math.sin(w * t - phi);
  i ? ctx.lineTo(tx(t), ty(x)) : ctx.moveTo(tx(t), ty(x));
}
ctx.stroke();

// 4. optional envelope, same loop with R(t) and -R(t)
// 5. pointer guide line, drawn last so it sits on top

Every slider does the same thing: change one number in the state object, then run this whole routine again from scratch. There is no incremental redraw and no animation loop in tab 1 — a full repaint at 60 frames a second is cheap for a few thousand line segments, which is why dragging feels continuous.
