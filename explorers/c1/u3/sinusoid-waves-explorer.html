Three ways to say “how fast”

Period, frequency and angular frequency are the same fact in different units — like quoting a journey as 2 hours, or 0.5 trips per hour, or 30 miles per hour.

T = seconds per cycle  |  f = cycles per second  |  ω = radians per second

Going between them is only unit bookkeeping:

  • f = 1/T — reciprocals: 4 s per cycle means 0.25 cycles per second.
  • ω = 2πf — multiply cycles-per-second by 2π radians-per-cycle.
  • ω = 2π/T — combine the two.

Why radians rather than cycles?

Because cos and sin take angles, not cycles. If you wrote cos(f·t) the input would be in cycles and the function would repeat every 1/f only by accident. Multiplying by 2π converts cycles into the radians the cosine expects — that 2π is the entire reason ω exists.

What a phasor is, and why it works

A phasor is just an arrow of fixed length A pinned at the origin, turning steadily at ω radians per second. Nothing more.

The bicycle-valve analogy. Watch the valve cap on a rolling bicycle wheel from directly behind. The cap goes round and round, but all you see is it moving up and down. That up-and-down shadow of a circular motion is a sinusoid. The wheel is the phasor.

Why the shadow is exactly A cos θ

Put the arrow tip on a circle of radius A at angle θ. By the definition of cosine and sine in a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the arrow:

horizontal reach = A cos θ     vertical reach = A sin θ

Now let θ grow with time as θ = ωt − φ. The horizontal reach becomes A cos(ωt − φ) — which is precisely the wave. So the wave is not like a rotation; it is one, seen edge-on.

What each ingredient becomes on the circle

  • A → the radius of the circle.
  • ω → the turning rate. One lap = 2π radians = one period.
  • φ → a head start (or handicap): where the arrow was pointing at t = 0. The pink arc between the two arrows.
  • t₀ → how many seconds until the arrow reaches the θ = 0 mark.

Which way does it turn?

That depends on where you measure θ from, and the checkbox in the inputs panel switches between the two:

  • From 12 o'clock (default here): the arrow's height is A cos θ, matching the wave's vertical axis, so the orange connector is honest. Increasing θ then sweeps clockwise.
  • From 3 o'clock (textbook): increasing θ sweeps counter-clockwise, the usual convention for eiθ. But then the wave value is the arrow's horizontal reach, not its height.

Both draw the identical wave — only the starting mark differs. Toggle the checkbox and watch the blue curve stay exactly where it is.

What phase lag really is

The runner analogy. Two runners go round the same circular track at the same speed. They never catch each other, so the gap between them is fixed. You can quote that gap in two ways: as an angle around the track (that is φ), or as seconds — how long after the first runner passes a post the second one does (that is t₀). Same gap, two units.

What φ is not. It is not a time, not a distance, and not something you measure off the vertical axis. It is an angle, and it never changes as time passes — it is baked into the formula.

Reading φ off the picture

  1. Find the peak of the grey reference curve nearest t = 0. The reference is A cos(ωt), whose peak is at t = 0, because cos is largest when its input is 0.
  2. Find the matching peak of the blue curve. It sits later (if φ > 0).
  3. The horizontal gap between those two peaks is the time lag t₀, in seconds — the teal arrow.
  4. Convert that gap to an angle by asking what fraction of a whole cycle it is, then multiplying by 2π:   φ = 2π · (t₀/T) = ω t₀.

Why the formula puts φ where it does

The cosine peaks when its input is zero. In A cos(ωt − φ) that happens when

ωt − φ = 0  ⇒  t = φ/ω = t₀

So φ is precisely the amount you must subtract from the angle to delay the peak from t = 0 to t = t₀. The minus sign is what makes a positive φ mean “later”.

With your current numbers

Why quote it as an angle at all? Because the angle version is independent of speed. Change ω and t₀ changes, but φ does not — a quarter-cycle delay is a quarter cycle whether the oscillation takes 2 seconds or 2 hours. Watch it on tab 2: hold φ and move T.

Turning an angle into seconds

Two routes to the same number, both worth knowing.

Route 1 — divide by the rate

ω tells you radians per second. To convert radians into seconds you divide:

t₀ = φ ÷ ω   =   rad ÷ (rad/s)   =   s

The units cancel correctly, which is a good check that the formula is the right way up.

Route 2 — fraction of a cycle

Ask what fraction of a full turn φ is, then take that fraction of the period:

t₀ = (φ / 2π) × T

These agree because T = 2π/ω. Route 2 is often quicker mentally: φ = π/2 is a quarter turn, so t₀ is a quarter of the period, whatever the period happens to be.

Your numbers, both ways

Reading it backwards. If you can measure the shift off a graph in seconds, you get the angle by multiplying: φ = ω t₀. That is how you would extract φ from experimental data.

Why the pattern moves at ω/k

Pick a feature to follow — say a crest. A crest sits wherever the phase inside the cosine equals zero:

k x − ω t + φ = 0

Solve for the crest's position at time t:

x = (ω t − φ)/k = (ω/k) t − φ/k

That is a straight line in t with slope ω/k. So the crest advances at constant speed v = ω/k, and the φ term only sets where it started. Flip the sign of the ωt term and the slope goes negative — the wave runs the other way.

The intuitive version

In one period T the whole pattern slides forward by exactly one wavelength λ. Speed is distance over time, so v = λ/T. Substituting λ = 2π/k and T = 2π/ω, the 2π's cancel and you are back to ω/k.
