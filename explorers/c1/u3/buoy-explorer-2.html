The set-up (as stated in the course)

A cylindrical buoy is to be placed in a pond. We begin with the bottom of the buoy touching the surface of the water, then release it. At this point it is acted on by two forces: the downward force of gravity mg and the upward buoyancy force equal to the weight of water displaced (Archimedes' principle).

To model this buoy we need its geometry. It has radius r (cm), height h (cm) and density ρb = 0.5 g/cm³. (The density of water is ρw = 1 g/cm³.) Suppose further that the buoy descends symmetrically into the water — it does not rock from side to side.

Two forces act: gravity pulls down, and the water pushes up with a force equal to the weight of the water the buoy displaces (Archimedes). Drag the depth slider and watch them fight. The derivation below uses your slider values for the numbers, and the course's symbols for the algebra.

  • Slide x until the net force reads 0. That depth is where the buoy floats.
  • Change the radius r. Every force changes, but ẍ does not — r cancels.
  • Set ρb = 0.9: the buoy floats much deeper (xeq = 0.9h).

Net force as a function of depth

The net force is a straight line in x that crosses zero at the floating depth. Above that depth the buoy is pushed down, below it pushed up. A force that always points back toward one position is a restoring force — exactly what makes an oscillator.

Deriving the equation, step by step

    Common slips. (1) Forgetting which way is positive — here down is positive, so gravity is +mg and buoyancy is negative. (2) Using the whole height h for the submerged volume: only the part below the surface, of height x, displaces water. (3) Leaving πr² in the answer — it multiplies both sides and cancels.

      Solving for R, step by step

        Whatever ρb you choose, the buoy changes shape (taller and thinner, or shorter and fatter) but the radius R never moves. That is the algebra telling you the density cancels: the bobbing frequency only ever "knew" the mass and the cross-section.
        Common slips. (1) Mixing units — g = 9.8 m/s² with a mass in kg gives R in metres (0.101 m); the course wants cm, so use 981 cm/s² and 50,000 g throughout. (2) Using the frequency 4/10 = 0.4 Hz where ωn (rad/s) is needed: ωn = 2π·0.4 = 2.513 rad/s. (3) Trying to find h and ρb separately — you can't, and you don't need to: only their product hρb is determined.

          Summary

          The whole story on one page

          1 · ForcesGravity +mg down, buoyancy −ρwπr²xg up (Archimedes).
          Newtonmẍ = FG + FB ⇒ ẍ = g − (2g/h)x. Radius cancels.
          2 · SolveLinear, constant coefficients: x = h/2 + c₁cos ωnt + c₂sin ωnt.
          Fit the startx(0)=0, ẋ(0)=0 ⇒ x = (h/2)(1 − cos ωnt): T ≈ 2 s, A = 1 m.
          3 · ReverseMeasure T and M ⇒ R = √(Mωn²/πgρw) = 10.1 cm.
          4 · CritiqueNo damping term ⇒ bobs forever. Real water adds −bẋ.

          One idea to keep. Every "floating thing bobbing" problem is a mass on a spring in disguise: the spring constant is (weight of water displaced per unit depth) = ρwgπr², the mass is ρbπr²h, and ωn² = k/m. That is why r cancels and only the ratio ρw/(ρbh) survives.
