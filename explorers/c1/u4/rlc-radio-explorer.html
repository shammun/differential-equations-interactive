What you are looking at

Three different jobs, three different relationships to the same current:

  • the resistor cares about the current right now,
  • the inductor cares about how fast the current is changing,
  • the capacitor cares about how much current has accumulated.

Value now, derivative, integral. That is the whole reason a circuit obeys a second-order differential equation.

The punchline

All three answers have the same left-hand side:

$$L\ddot{V}_\bullet + R\dot{V}_\bullet + \tfrac{1}{C}V_\bullet = (\text{something})\,V$$

That left side is the circuit itself — the parts and how they are wired. It does not care which voltage you decided to watch.

Only the right-hand side changes, and it changes in a way you can almost guess:

  • watching the resistor → $R\dot V$  R·D
  • watching the inductor → $L\ddot V$  L·D^2
  • watching the capacitor → $\tfrac1C V$  1/C

Each output gets driven through its own component, so the input arrives wearing that component's operator: multiply for $R$, differentiate twice for $L$, scale by $1/C$ for the capacitor.

Those bracketed answers are exactly what the homework boxes want: R, 1/C, and then L*D^2 for the inductor problem and 1/C for the capacitor problem.

The dial reading

The antenna picks up every station at once. The loop only passes current easily at one frequency, so only one station reaches the speaker loudly. Turning the knob changes C, which slides the peak along the dial.

A smaller R makes the peak taller and narrower: more selective, but the speaker also rings longer after the signal stops.

Walk-through

Symbol $Q$  ·  unit the coulomb (C)

Charge is the stuff that moves. It is a property some particles have; in a wire the movers are electrons. One coulomb is about $6.24\times10^{18}$ electrons — the amount that passes through a 1-amp torch bulb every second.

The two facts you actually need

  • It is conserved. Charge is never created or destroyed inside the circuit, only relocated. In a single loop there is nowhere for it to pile up, which is exactly why one symbol $I(t)$ describes the current everywhere.
  • It is what a capacitor stores. The capacitor in this circuit works by holding charge on its plates: $Q = C\,V_C$. Nothing else in the circuit stores charge.

In the plumbing picture

Charge is the water itself. Not the flow, not the pressure — the litres.

Charge rarely appears explicitly in the differential equation. It hides inside the capacitor law, and the book eliminates it immediately by differentiating $Q = CV_C$ into $I = C\dot V_C$.

Symbol $I(t)$  ·  unit the ampere (A) = coulombs per second

Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point:

$$I = \frac{dQ}{dt}$$

If 0.5 coulombs cross a given cross-section of wire in 0.25 seconds, the current there is $0.5/0.25 = 2$ amps.

Why it carries a sign

Current has a direction, so before you can write a number you have to pick which way counts as positive. The book picks clockwise and draws the arrow $I$ on the left wire. If the charge is actually running anticlockwise at that moment, $I(t)$ simply comes out negative. Nothing about the equations changes.

Why there is only one of them here

This is a series circuit — a single loop with no branches. Charge cannot accumulate in the wire and has nowhere else to go, so the current in the top wire equals the current in the bottom wire at every instant. That is what lets the whole circuit be described by one unknown function.

In the plumbing picture: litres per second through the pipe.

Symbol $V$  ·  unit the volt (V) = joules per coulomb

Voltage is energy per unit of charge. It is not a substance and it does not flow. It is always a difference between two points — like height, which only means something relative to some other height.

Gain and drop

Gravity is the honest analogy, and it is the one drawn on the left.

  • The antenna, driven by the radio wave, lifts charge to higher energy. That is a voltage gain of $V(t)$ volts.
  • Each of the other three parts lets charge fall back down, taking energy out. That is a voltage drop.

Go all the way round the loop and you must end up back where you started, so the total lift equals the total descent:

$$V = V_L + V_R + V_C$$

That is Kirchhoff's voltage law, and it is nothing more than energy conservation written for a closed path.

A worked number

Pushing 2 coulombs through a 6-volt drop releases $2\times6 = 12$ joules. Do that in one second and you are dissipating 12 watts — power is $P = VI$. In this circuit the power delivered to $R$ is what comes out of the speaker as sound, which is why the book declares $V_R$ to be the system response.

Symbol $R$  ·  unit the ohm ($\Omega$) = volts per amp  ·  drawn as a zigzag

A resistor is a part that opposes flow and converts the electrical energy irreversibly into some other form. In an ordinary circuit that means heat; here the resistor is the speaker, so it comes out as sound.

Its defining law

$$V_R = R\,I$$

This is Ohm's law, and it is a definition: a resistor is by definition the part whose drop is proportional to the current through it. The constant of proportionality $R$ measures how strongly it obstructs.

The property that matters for the ODE

The resistor is memoryless. Its drop depends only on the current at this very instant — not on what the current was doing a moment ago, not on how fast it is changing. Double the current now, double the drop now, instantly and in step.

Worked number. $R = 3\ \Omega$ and $I = 2$ A give a drop of 6 V, dissipating $6\times2 = 12$ W.

In the mechanical dictionary the resistor is the dashpot: a damping constant $b$. Both act on the first derivative, both drain energy out of the system, and both are the reason oscillations die away.

Symbol $L$  ·  unit the henry (H) = volt-seconds per amp  ·  drawn as a coil

An inductor is a coil of wire. Current through the coil creates a magnetic field around it; if that current changes, the changing field pushes back against the change. That push-back is the voltage drop.

Its defining law

$$V_L = L\,\dot I$$

The drop is proportional to the rate of change of the current, not the current itself.

What that implies

  • A perfectly steady current through an ideal coil costs zero volts, however large that current is.
  • Current through an inductor cannot jump instantaneously — that would need an infinite voltage. The coil is the inertia of the circuit.
  • The drop reverses sign the moment the current stops rising and starts falling.

Worked number. $L = 0.5$ H with the current climbing at 4 A/s gives $0.5\times4 = 2$ V. If the current is falling at 4 A/s instead, the drop is $-2$ V. If the current is held at 100 A but steady, the drop is 0.

Energy is not lost here, only parked: the coil stores $\tfrac12 L I^2$ in its magnetic field and gives it back.

In the mechanical dictionary the inductor is the mass. Both resist change rather than motion, both act on the second derivative of the response, and both store energy rather than dissipate it.

Symbol $C$  ·  unit the farad (F) = coulombs per volt  ·  drawn as two parallel plates

A capacitor is two conducting plates separated by a gap. Charge piles up on one plate and an equal amount leaves the other. No charge ever crosses the gap — and yet current flows in the wires on both sides, because charge arriving at one plate pushes charge off the other.

Its defining law

The charge stored is proportional to the voltage across the plates, $Q = C\,V_C$. Differentiate that, using $I = \dot Q$:

$$C\,\dot V_C = I \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad V_C = \frac{1}{C}\int I\,dt$$

What that implies

  • The capacitor has memory. Its voltage is not set by the current now but by the entire history of current that has flowed into it.
  • Its voltage cannot jump instantaneously — that would need infinite current.
  • A small $C$ is a stiff capacitor: little charge produces a lot of back-voltage.

Worked number. $C = 0.25$ F carrying $I = 1$ A gives $\dot V_C = 1/0.25 = 4$ V/s. Its voltage climbs four volts every second for as long as that current keeps flowing.

Like the coil it stores rather than dissipates, holding $\tfrac12 C V_C^2$ in the electric field between the plates.

In the mechanical dictionary the capacitor is the spring, with spring constant $k = 1/C$. Note the reciprocal: a small capacitor is a stiff spring. Both push back in proportion to accumulated displacement.

Everything in this circuit is built from three ideas, and the differential equation falls out of how they combine.

Charge, current, voltage

Charge $Q$ is the stuff. Current $I = \dot Q$ is how fast it moves. Voltage is the energy each unit of charge picks up or gives up — a difference between two points, like height.

Only two of these ever appear in the final equation. Charge is eliminated early, as soon as $Q = CV_C$ is differentiated into $C\dot V_C = I$.

The three components, side by side

Each part is defined by how its voltage drop relates to the same current $I$:

  • resistor  $V_R = RI$  —  the current now
  • inductor  $V_L = L\dot I$  —  the derivative of the current
  • capacitor  $V_C = \frac1C\int I\,dt$  —  the integral of the current

Value, derivative, integral of one single function. Add those three together, set the sum equal to the source, and differentiate once to clear the integral: you have a second-order ODE. There is no deeper reason than that.

The dictionary to the mechanical case

Inductance is mass (resists change, stores energy). Resistance is the damping constant (drains energy). $1/C$ is the spring constant (pushes back on accumulated displacement). Every result you already derived for $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx$ transfers across unchanged.

Before any differential equation, four words.

Charge, current, voltage

Charge is the stuff that moves. Current $I(t)$ is how fast it moves — amps are coulombs per second. Voltage is the push: the energy each unit of charge gains or gives up in passing through something. Volts are joules per coulomb.

A plumbing picture that is accurate enough to be useful: current is the litres per second flowing through the pipe, voltage is the pressure difference across a section of pipe.

Why one current for the whole circuit

This circuit is a single loop — a series circuit. There is nowhere for charge to pile up or branch off, so the current in the wire at the top is the same number as the current in the wire at the bottom. That is why the text can write one symbol $I(t)$ and be done.

Choosing "clockwise" is just choosing which direction counts as positive. If the real current is running the other way, $I(t)$ comes out negative. Nothing breaks.

Gain versus drop

The antenna, driven by the incoming radio wave, lifts charge to higher energy: a voltage gain of $V(t)$ volts. Everything else takes energy back out: a voltage drop. Kirchhoff's voltage law is just energy bookkeeping around the loop — go all the way around and you must return to where you started:

$$V = V_L + V_R + V_C$$

Same logic as walking a closed hiking loop: total climb equals total descent.

The three components

Resistor $\;V_R = RI$. It obstructs flow and dumps the energy — here as sound, since the resistor is the speaker. Its drop depends on the current at this instant. Double the current now, double the drop now. With $R=3$ and $I=2$ amps, the drop is $6$ volts.

Inductor $\;V_L = L\dot I$. A coil of wire storing energy in a magnetic field. It is the inertia of the circuit: it does not object to current, it objects to changing current. Steady current through an ideal coil costs zero volts. With $L=0.5$ and the current climbing at $4$ amps per second, the drop is $2$ volts — and it flips sign the moment the current starts falling.

Capacitor $\;C\dot V_C = I$, i.e. $\dot V_C = I/C$. Two plates with a gap; charge collects on them. Think of a rubber membrane across a pipe: flow stretches it and it pushes back harder the more it has stretched. Its voltage is not set by the current now but by the accumulated current: $V_C = \frac1C\int I\,dt$. With $C = 0.25$ and $I = 1$ amp, $\dot V_C = 4$, so its voltage climbs 4 volts every second for as long as that current keeps flowing.

Value, derivative, integral of the same $I$. Add them up and set the sum equal to $V$, and you are one differentiation away from a second-order ODE. That is the entire trick.

Reading the plot on the left

Drive $I(t)=\cos\omega t$ through each part. The peaks are $R$, $\;\omega L$, and $1/(\omega C)$. Notice what happens as you drag $\omega$: the coil gets harder to push at high frequency, the capacitor gets harder to push at low frequency, and the resistor does not care. Those two opposite trends cross at one frequency — keep that in mind for tab 4.

Here the loop runs for real. Nothing is a formula for a sine wave; the state is integrated forward in time from the two equations that actually define the circuit.

What is being integrated

Take Kirchhoff's law $V = L\dot I + RI + V_C$ and solve for the derivative:

$$\dot I = \frac{V(t) - RI - V_C}{L},\qquad \dot V_C = \frac{I}{C}$$

Two state variables, $I$ and $V_C$, and the rest is arithmetic: $V_R = RI$, and $V_L = L\dot I$, which is whatever is left over after the other two drops are subtracted from $V$. The right-hand panel shows exactly that arithmetic at the current instant, so you can check the bookkeeping line by line.

Things worth doing

  • Set $R$ very small and press Restart from rest. The current sloshes back and forth between coil and capacitor almost forever — energy trading between magnetic field and electric field. That is an undamped oscillator, the electrical twin of a mass on a spring.
  • Raise $R$. The oscillation dies out. $R$ is the dashpot.
  • Press Tune to resonance ($\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$) and watch $V_R$ grow far taller than the source $V$ itself. The circuit is not creating energy; the coil and the capacitor are handing the same energy back and forth, and the source only has to top up what the speaker consumes.
  • Watch $V_L$ and $V_C$ at resonance: they are large and almost exactly out of phase, so they nearly cancel. What is left over is $V_R$, which is why $V_R \approx V$ at resonance.

The mechanical twin

Put the two equations side by side:

$$m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = \text{forcing}\qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad L\ddot V_\bullet + R\dot V_\bullet + \tfrac1C V_\bullet = \text{forcing}$$

Mass $\leftrightarrow$ inductance (both resist change). Damping $\leftrightarrow$ resistance (both drain energy). Spring constant $\leftrightarrow$ $1/C$ (a small capacitor is a stiff spring — it fights back hard after only a little charge). Every result from the spring-mass-dashpot unit of the course transfers over unchanged.

The derivation looks fiddly, but there is one idea driving every move.

The obstacle

Kirchhoff's law $V = V_L + V_R + V_C$ mixes three quantities that relate to $I$ in three different ways. You cannot just substitute and be done, because the capacitor is defined by $C\dot V_C = I$ — a derivative, not a plain multiplication.

The strategy

Pick the one variable you actually want to watch, then make every other term wear that variable's clothing. You are allowed exactly two tools:

  • differentiate the whole equation (legal: both sides are functions of $t$),
  • multiply the whole equation by a constant ($L$, or $R$, or $1/C$).

Which tool and how many times is decided entirely by where the derivatives sit in your target's definition.

Worked out for the coil

Target $V_L = L\dot I$. That definition eats one derivative of $I$, so $V_L$ is "one derivative ahead" of $I$. To turn $\;RI\;$ into something containing $V_L$, you need an $\dot I$ — so differentiate. To turn $\;\frac1C\!\int\! I\;$ into $V_L$, you need to climb two derivatives — so differentiate twice in total. And $L\dot I$ only becomes exactly $V_L$ if the factor $L$ is present, so multiply through by $L$.

That is the entire plan: differentiate twice, multiply by $L$. The steps on the left are just that plan executed carefully.

Worked out for the capacitor

Target $V_C$, which sits one derivative behind $I$ (since $\dot V_C = I/C$). So this time no extra differentiation is needed at the start — substituting turns $I$ into $\dot V_C$ and $\dot I$ into $\ddot V_C$ all by itself. You only need the factor $1/C$ to be present, so multiply through by $1/C$. Fewer moves, same destination.

If you ever forget the recipe, count derivatives. $V_C$ is one behind $I$, $V_R$ is level with $I$, $V_L$ is one ahead. Differentiate the whole law until your target's lowest-order term lands on plain $V_\bullet$, then multiply by whatever constant makes the substitution exact.

Checking the answer

Every version must come out as $L\ddot V_\bullet + R\dot V_\bullet + \frac1C V_\bullet$ on the left. If your left-hand side has different coefficients, you multiplied by the wrong constant. The right-hand side should be the input $V$ acted on by your target component's own operator: $RD$, $LD^2$, or $1/C$.

This is the part the textbook is building towards, and it explains why anyone would model a radio this way at all.

Reactance: the frequency-dependent resistance

Drive the loop with a pure sine at frequency $\omega$. Feeding a sinusoidal current of amplitude 1 through each part costs a peak voltage of

  • resistor: $R$ — flat, indifferent to frequency,
  • inductor: $\omega L$ — rises with frequency,
  • capacitor: $1/(\omega C)$ — falls with frequency.

The last two also act in opposite directions in time: the coil's voltage peaks a quarter cycle early, the capacitor's a quarter cycle late. So they partly cancel, and their net cost is $\;\omega L - 1/(\omega C)$.

Resonance

At one special frequency that difference is zero:

$$\omega L = \frac{1}{\omega C}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$

There, the coil and capacitor cancel each other exactly and the loop behaves as if only the resistor were there. Current is at its maximum, and since loudness is proportional to $V_R = RI$, the speaker is at its loudest. The amplitude of $V_R$ relative to $V$ is

$$\left|\frac{V_R}{V}\right| = \frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}}$$

which equals 1 at $\omega_0$ and falls off on both sides — a band-pass filter.

Tuning

The antenna receives every broadcaster in town simultaneously, superimposed. Only the frequencies near $\omega_0$ produce appreciable current. Turning the tuning knob is physically turning a variable capacitor, moving $\omega_0$ onto the station you want. With $L = 250\ \mu\text{H}$ and $C = 100\ \text{pF}$, $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC} \approx 6.3\times10^{6}$ rad/s, i.e. about 1007 kHz — a normal AM dial position.

Sharpness

How narrow the peak is depends on $Q = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{L/C}$. Large $Q$ (small $R$) means sharp selectivity — neighbouring stations are rejected — but also a long ringing transient, which smears fast changes in the signal. In oscillator language, high $Q$ is lightly damped. The engineering trade-off and the ODE trade-off are literally the same trade-off.

Start where all three derivations start: the loop law. Gain at the antenna equals the sum of the drops.

$$V = V_L + V_R + V_C$$

Right now this has three unknown voltages in it. The definitions of the components will let us write all of them in terms of the single current $I$.

The three defining relations. Notice the derivative in the capacitor one — that is the awkward term.

$$V_L = L\dot I,\qquad V_R = R I,\qquad C\dot V_C = I$$

Two of these give a voltage directly. The third gives the derivative of a voltage. So substituting straight into the loop law would leave an integral behind.

Fix that by differentiating the whole loop law once. Now every term is a voltage rate, and $\dot V_C$ is something we have a formula for.

$$\dot V = \dot V_L + \dot V_R + \dot V_C$$

Substitute: $\dot V_L = L\ddot I$, $\;\dot V_R = R\dot I$, $\;\dot V_C = I/C$. Everything is now in terms of $I$ alone.

$$\dot V = L\ddot I + R\dot I + \tfrac{1}{C} I$$

This is already a perfectly good ODE — for the current. But the question asked about the speaker, so keep going.

We want $V_R$, and $V_R = RI$. Multiplying every term by $R$ makes each $I$ appear as $RI$.

$$R\dot V = L\,(R\ddot I) + R\,(R\dot I) + \tfrac{1}{C}(R I)$$

Now read off the substitutions: $RI = V_R$, and differentiating that, $R\dot I = \dot V_R$ and $R\ddot I = \ddot V_R$.

$$L\ddot V_R + R\dot V_R + \tfrac{1}{C} V_R = R\dot V$$

Standard form: response on the left, input on the right. In operator notation the right side is $RD\,V$.

Same starting point, then differentiate once, exactly as before.

$$\dot V_L + \dot V_R + \dot V_C = \dot V$$

Our target is $V_L = L\dot I$, so somewhere a factor of $L$ has to appear on every term. Put it there now.

Multiply both sides by $L$.

$$L\dot V_L + L\dot V_R + L\dot V_C = L\dot V$$

Substitute the two easy ones: $\dot V_R = R\dot I$ and $\dot V_C = I/C$.

$$L\dot V_L + LR\dot I + L\tfrac{1}{C} I = L\dot V$$

Look at the last two terms. We need them to become $V_L$ and $\dot V_L$, but $V_L = L\dot I$ — and the terms here contain $I$ and $\dot I$, one derivative short. So differentiate again.

Differentiate every term once more.

$$L\ddot V_L + LR\ddot I + L\tfrac{1}{C}\dot I = L\ddot V$$

Now the substitutions land exactly. Since $V_L = L\dot I$, we also have $\dot V_L = L\ddot I$. Group the constants so the $L$ sits next to the $I$-derivative:

$$LR\ddot I = R\,(L\ddot I) = R\,\dot V_L,\qquad \tfrac{L}{C}\dot I = \tfrac{1}{C}(L\dot I) = \tfrac{1}{C} V_L$$

Put them back in:

$$L\ddot V_L + R\dot V_L + \tfrac{1}{C} V_L = L\ddot V$$

So the boxes are: coefficient of $\dot V_L$ is R, coefficient of $V_L$ is 1/C, and the operator acting on $V$ is L*D^2.

This time do not differentiate first. Start from the plain loop law.

$$V_L + V_R + V_C = V$$

Reason: $V_C$ already sits one derivative behind $I$, so the substitutions will supply the derivatives for free.

Our target carries the constant $1/C$ (from $\dot V_C = I/C$), so multiply through by $1/C$.

$$\tfrac{V_L}{C} + \tfrac{V_R}{C} + \tfrac{V_C}{C} = \tfrac{1}{C}V$$

Substitute $V_L = L\dot I$ and $V_R = RI$.

$$L\tfrac{\dot I}{C} + R\tfrac{I}{C} + \tfrac{1}{C}V_C = \tfrac{1}{C}V$$

Here is the payoff. The capacitor law says $I/C = \dot V_C$; differentiating it gives $\dot I / C = \ddot V_C$. Both pieces are already sitting there.

$$\tfrac{I}{C} = \dot V_C,\qquad \tfrac{\dot I}{C} = \ddot V_C$$

Substitute and you are done — no second differentiation needed anywhere.

$$L\ddot V_C + R\dot V_C + \tfrac{1}{C} V_C = \tfrac{1}{C} V$$

Boxes: R, 1/C, and the right-hand side 1/C times $V$ — no derivative on the input at all.
