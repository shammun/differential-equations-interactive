18.03 · Unit 2 · complex roots

Where do the n-th roots sit, and why?

Every nonzero complex number has exactly n different n-th roots. They all share one modulus and sit at equal angular spacing — a regular n-gon. This page derives that, then lets you move the number and watch the roots respond.

First — what does “root” mean here?

A root is a solution: a value that satisfies an equation. Saying w is a cube root of z means exactly

w3 = z

and nothing more. You verify a root the same way you verify any solution — substitute it and check.

It is the same word you already know from polynomials

Move everything to one side:

w3 = z  ⇔  w3 − z = 0

The right-hand form is a polynomial in w, with z a fixed constant. So “the cube roots of z” and “the roots of the polynomial w3 − z” name the same set of numbers. That also explains the count: the polynomial has degree 3, and a degree-n polynomial has exactly n roots over the complex numbers.

No root is more “the” answer than the others. With z = 4 + 4i, all three of 1.72+0.46i, −1.26+1.26i and −0.46−1.72i cube to 4+4i. They are equally valid solutions. The one at k = 0 is only called principal by convention, because its angle is smallest.

Derive it one step at a time

We want every w with wn = z. Polar form turns this into two easy questions instead of one hard one.

The formula, and what each piece controls

wk = r1/n · ei(θ+2πk)/n   k = 0, 1, …, n−1

every root has this modulus

|wk| = r1/n

No k appears here, so all n roots are the same distance from the origin. They lie on one circle.

the angles are evenly spaced

arg wk = θ/n + k·(2π/n)

Consecutive roots differ by exactly 2π/n — one full turn cut into n equal slices.

Put those two facts together

Same distance from the origin, equal angles apart: that is the definition of a regular n-gon centred at the origin. Finding roots is really finding one corner and then stepping round the polygon.

Why exactly n and not more: try k = n. The angle becomes θ/n + 2π, which is the same direction as k = 0. From there the list repeats forever. So k = 0 through n−1 gives every root exactly once.

The one thing that surprises people

The modulus of a root is r1/n, and taking a root always drags the modulus toward 1, never past it:

If the number has…then its roots have…meaning
|z| > 11 < r1/n < rroots sit on a circle inside the number, but still outside the unit circle
|z| = 1r1/n = 1roots stay on the unit circle — it is the fixed circle
|z| < 1r < r1/n < 1roots sit outside the number, but still inside the unit circle

Reason: r1/n = e(ln r)/n. Dividing ln r by n shrinks it toward 0, and ln r = 0 is exactly r = 1. Tab 2 shows this on a ruler.

Every root, and exactly how it is computed

Observation 1 — the modulus of a root

The course page states three cases. All three are one sentence in disguise: taking an n-th root moves the modulus toward 1.

|z| = 1 ⇒ |z1/n| = 1    |z| > 1 ⇒ |z1/n| > 1    |z| < 1 ⇒ |z1/n| < 1

Each root keeps the same side of the unit circle as z — it never crosses over — but it always ends up nearer to it. Raising to the power 1/n can't change the sign of ln r, only shrink its size.

Observations 2 and 3 — which circle is closer

These two bullets say the roots always lie between the unit circle and z's own circle, on z's side. Concretely, with |z| = 8 and n = 3:

1 < 81/3 = 2 < 8

and with |z| = 1/8:

1/8 < (1/8)1/3 = 1/2 < 1

Increasing n pulls the roots closer still. In the limit of large n the root circle collapses onto the unit circle, whatever |z| was.

Observation 4 — z on the positive real axis

For what values of n does z have a positive real root?

Positive real axis means θ = 0. The root angles are then

(0 + 2πk)/n = 2πk/n

and k = 0 gives angle 0 — pointing straight along the positive real axis. That works no matter what n is.

Answer: every n. The root is r1/n, the ordinary positive real n-th root you already know from real numbers. It is always in the list, sitting at k = 0.

Observation 5 — z on the negative real axis

For what values of n does z have a negative real root?

Negative real axis means θ = π. The root angles are

(π + 2πk)/n

and we need one of them to point at angle π as well (that is what negative real means). Setting them equal, and allowing a whole number m of extra turns:

(π + 2πk)/n = π + 2πm  ⇒  1 + 2k = n(1 + 2m)

The left side is odd. The factor 1 + 2m is odd. So n must be odd too — and if n is odd, taking m = 0 and k = (n−1)/2 works.

Answer: exactly the odd n.

Example 1 — the square roots of i

Step 1. Put i in polar form. The point i is at (0, 1), so r = 1 and θ = π/2:

i = 1·eiπ/2

Step 2. Apply the formula with n = 2.

wk = 11/2 ei(π/2 + 2πk)/2 = ei(π/4 + πk),   k = 0, 1

Step 3. Write out both.

w0 = eiπ/4 = cos 45° + i sin 45° = 1/√2 + i/√2

w1 = ei5π/4 = −1/√2 − i/√2

Both have modulus 1, because |i| = 1 and roots of a unit-modulus number stay on the unit circle. They are 180° apart, which is 2π/2 — the spacing rule for n = 2. Numerically 1/√2 ≈ 0.71.

Check. (1/√2 + i/√2)2 = 1/2 + 2·(1/√2)(i/√2) + i2/2 = 1/2 + i − 1/2 = i ✓

Example 2 — solve z5 = z

Step 1. Don't divide yet. Move everything to one side and factor, so no solution is lost:

z5 − z = 0  ⇒  z(z4 − 1) = 0

A product is zero when a factor is zero, giving z = 0 or z4 = 1. (This is why the problem tells you z = 0 is one solution — dividing by z straight away would have thrown it away.)

Step 2. Solve z4 = 1 with the root formula. Here 1 = 1·ei·0, so r = 1, θ = 0, n = 4:

wk = 11/4 ei(0 + 2πk)/4 = eiπk/2,   k = 0, 1, 2, 3

Solutions: z = 0, 1, i, −1, −i — five in all, as a degree-5 equation should have. The four nonzero ones are the corners of a square on the unit circle.

These four are the fourth roots of unity. Roots of unity are always the evenly spaced corners of a polygon starting at 1, because θ = 0 puts the k = 0 corner on the positive real axis.
