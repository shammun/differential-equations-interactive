Complex numbers · Polar form

From x + iy to reiθ

A complex number is a point in the plane. You can name that point two ways: by walking along the axes (x right, y up), or by aiming and shooting (turn by angle θ, then go distance r). Polar form is just the second way of naming the same point. Below we convert the four numbers from the figure — −i,  1 + i,  1 − i,  −1 + i√3 — one honest step at a time.

The recipe (works for every complex number). Everything on this page is these four moves, repeated with different numbers.

Read off x and y.

Write the number as z = x + iy. The real part x is the horizontal step; the coefficient of i is the vertical step y. Plot the point.

Find the distance r — Pythagoras.

The legs x and y form a right triangle whose hypotenuse runs from the origin to the point, so

r = |z| = √(x² + y²)
Find the angle θ — trig, plus a look at the picture.

In that same triangle, tan θ = y/x. Take the arctangent to get a candidate angle, then check which quadrant the point actually sits in and correct if needed (see the warning below).

Assemble, then check.

Since x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ:

z = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = reiθ

Multiplying back out must return exactly x + iy — if it doesn't, the angle is wrong.

The one place people slip: arctan is quadrant-blind.
A calculator's tan⁻¹ only answers between −π/2 and π/2 (quadrants I and IV). If your point is in quadrant II or III, the raw arctan points at the wrong spot — add or subtract π to land on the true direction. You'll see this bite (and get fixed) in the −1 + i√3 example.

Two useful angle facts before we start:

① Angles are directions, so they repeat every full turn. θ and θ + 2π point the same way — that's why 3π/2 (270° counterclockwise) and −π/2 (90° clockwise) describe the identical direction "straight down".

② Below the real axis, angles come out negative (or, if you prefer, bigger than π). Reflecting a point across the real axis — taking the conjugate z̄, exactly as in Fig 1 — keeps r and flips the sign of θ. Watch 1 + i vs 1 − i.

Handy exact values (they appear constantly): cos π/4 = sin π/4 = √22,  cos π/3 = 12,  sin π/3 = √32.
